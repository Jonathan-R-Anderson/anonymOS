## 28. Workstream 1 — Long-Term Intersection Resistance

### 28.1 The arithmetic that defines the problem

Guard rotation is repeated sampling. A client with `g` guard slots rotating every
`R` days draws `n = g · T/R` guard selections over `T` days, and

```text
P(at least one hostile guard within T)  =  1 − (1 − f)^(g·T/R)
```

At AXON's current parameters — `g = 2`, `R = 45 d` (§8.5) — over one year:

| `f` | `n = 16` draws | `g=1, R=120 d` (n=3) | `g=2, R=45 d` **(current)** | `g=3, R=30 d` (n=36) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | | 0.030 | **0.149** | 0.303 |
| 0.05 | | 0.143 | **0.560** | 0.842 |
| 0.10 | | 0.271 | **0.815** | 0.977 |
| 0.20 | | 0.488 | **0.972** | 0.9998 |

**The current parameters are the worst column but one.** At a 5 % adversary, a
client passes through a hostile guard within a year with probability 0.56. This
is not a subtle finding and it is not new — it is the calculation that moved Tor
to a single long-lived guard — and §8.5's choice of "deliberately between Tor and
I2P" was made without it being written down.

Two clarifications that stop this being over-read. A hostile guard is *not*
deanonymisation: the adversary also needs the other end, which is what makes `f`
appear twice in the full expression and is why P5 and P7 are coupled to this
workstream. And for a client, rotation is a genuine defence against a *different*
attack — a single permanent guard that is hostile is permanently hostile. The
trade is real; the current point on it was chosen by feel.

### 28.2 The service side is the urgent half

A client can go offline. A service that is always reachable cannot, and its
guards are therefore subject to unlimited repeated probing: an adversary who can
cause a service to build circuits on demand (by connecting to it) can force
guard-position sampling at a rate the adversary chooses rather than the schedule
chooses. This converts a slow intersection attack into an active one, and it is
the attack that has actually been used against onion services.

The defence is **layered guard sets (vanguards)**: the service pins not one layer
but three, at different rotation periods, so an adversary must compromise a path
through all three layers *before any of them rotates*.

```text
  Service ─► L1 (2 relays,  ~months)   ─► L2 (4 relays, ~weeks)
                                        ─► L3 (8 relays, ~days)  ─► RP

  discovery requires L1 ∧ L2 ∧ L3 simultaneously
  P(path fully hostile per attempt) ≈ f³   (vs f for a flat guard)
  and the adversary must re-achieve it after the fastest layer rotates
```

At `f = 0.05`, `f³ = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴` per attempt against `5 × 10⁻²` flat — roughly a
400× reduction in per-attempt success, and the rotation schedule caps how many
attempts a single compromise survives.

### 28.3 The seventeen fields

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Threat model** | Adversary controls `f` of relay-selection probability, persistent over months, logs all circuits it carries, can connect to a target service arbitrarily often to force circuit construction, and can observe descriptor fetches at the DHT position. Cannot break primitives; cannot observe the whole Internet |
| **Current AXON weakness** | `g=2, R=45 d` gives `P = 0.56` at `f = 0.05` over a year (§28.1); no layered guards; intro-point rotation on republish is a *schedule*, not a defence against forced sampling; no isolation-context definition that survives across restarts |

### 28.4 What this workstream does NOT establish

- It does not defend a persistently-online service against an adversary willing
  to observe for multiple years; it changes the constant, not the asymptote.
- It says nothing about correlation once both ends are observed — that is §32.
- The client-side target is deliberately absent. §28.6 explains why.
- Churn distributions are assumed. If the real network's churn differs materially
  from the simulated one, every number in §28.1 moves.

| Source | Classification | What changes in AXON | Property that does not transfer |

### 28.6 The client target is missing, deliberately

A defensible client-side `P_dea` target requires knowing the client's threat
horizon, and that varies by four orders of magnitude between a casual reader and
a targeted individual. Publishing a single number would be false precision.
Instead the workstream publishes the curve and requires the *deployment* to pick
its point — and §40 records that a client whose adversary observes for a year at
`f = 0.05` is, under every topology examined, likely to have used a hostile
guard. `RESEARCH REQUIRED` on whether a defensible universal target exists at all.

## 29. Workstream 2 — Abuse Handling Without Re-Centralisation

### 29.1 The finding that shapes this workstream

**There is no technical mechanism that removes content globally and is not also a
global censorship mechanism.** These are the same mechanism viewed from two
sides, and no amount of governance design separates them. The workstream's
purpose is therefore not to build global removal but to establish exactly where
technical enforcement ends and governance begins, to build everything on the
technical side of that line, and to state plainly that the other side is not an
engineering problem.

The line, stated once: **an operator may always refuse to serve. No party may
compel another operator to refuse.** Everything below follows from that.

### 29.2 Abuse is nine different problems

Treating "abuse" as one category is the error that produces global blocklists.

| Class | Who can act | Mechanism | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network attack (DoS, resource exhaustion) | Any node, unilaterally | Rate limits, puzzles (§9.6), bond-gated writes | **High** — the victim is the enforcer and has full authority over its own resources |
| Relay abuse (misrouting, dropping, lying) | Peers, via measurement | §35's receipts, slashing via `DisputeManager` | **Medium** — detectable, but attribution under collusion is hard |
| Storage abuse (junk, overuse, non-storage) | Storage node + auditors | Existing challenge/lying-holder machinery, quotas, bonds | **High** for capacity abuse; **low** for content |
| Fraud (payment, receipts) | Counterparties | Existing `DisputeManager`, bonded challenges | **High** — there is an injured party with evidence and incentive |
| Spam (unsolicited service traffic) | Service operator | Client puzzles, credentials, per-service policy | **High** — the service decides |
| Malware distribution | Storage operator, client | Local policy, signed advisories, client-side scanning | **Low** at network level, and any network-level answer is a content oracle |
| Illegal content | Operators, individually | Local policy + subscribed assertions | **Low**, and deliberately so — see §29.6 |
| Namespace abuse (phishing names) | Registrar, per namespace | Registrar policy (§12.0), mutual binding (§11.0.3) | **Medium** — bounded to one namespace by design |
| Governance abuse | Voters, fork right | §12.0a | **Low** — reactive |

**Five of nine have effective, purely local answers.** The workstream should
build those first and completely, because they cover the abuse classes that
actually threaten the network's operation, and they require no new trust
structure at all.

### 29.3 The mechanism: subscribable signed policy, composed locally

```text
   PolicyAssertion {
     issuer        Ed25519 pubkey of the publisher (not a network role)
     subject       ContentIdentity | ServiceIdentity | name | relay NodeIdentity
     verdict       DENY | QUARANTINE | ADVISE
     scope         STORE | SERVE | RELAY | RESOLVE | INTRODUCE
     evidence      ContentIdentity of a statement of reasons (may be sealed)
     issued, expires
     signature
   }
```

Composition is **per-operator and local**. An operator subscribes to zero or more
issuers and chooses a combining rule; the protocol defines the assertion format
and the evaluation order, and defines nothing about which issuers exist.

```text
   effective_policy(node)  =  local_rules
                           ⊕  Σ subscribed_issuers
   default combining rule: DENY wins within a scope; ADVISE never blocks
   conflicts across issuers: the operator's ordering decides; there is no
   global tiebreak, because a global tiebreak is a global authority
```

Four properties, all of which must hold or the design has become a blocklist:

1. **No issuer is privileged in the protocol.** There is no default subscription
   shipped in the software. A shipped default *is* the global list.
2. **Assertions are not routing.** A `DENY` changes what a node will serve, never
   what other nodes can reach. Denial is not deletion.
3. **Subscription is visible to the operator and to nobody else** — subscription
   lists are local, because a published subscription is a coercion target.
4. **Global removal requires capacity, not authority.** With erasure coding at
   6+3 across `r = 8` holders, removing an object requires denying it at more
   than `n − k = 3` holders — so the fraction of storage capacity a coalition
   must control to remove a *specific* object is computable, and §37 states it.

### 29.4 The seventeen fields

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Threat model** | Two opposed adversaries: an abuser exploiting non-enforcement; a censor exploiting enforcement, including a censor who becomes the dominant policy publisher by legitimate means |
| **Current AXON weakness** | R5 refuses a global blocklist and stops there. No assertion format, no evidence format, no composition semantics, no measurement of what a coalition can remove |
| **Security objective** | (a) An operator enforces a subscribed policy within a stated propagation latency. (b) The fraction of storage capacity required for global removal of a specific object is computed and is greater than a stated floor. (c) No shipped default subscription exists — a build-time assertion, testable |
| **Candidate mechanisms** | Signed assertions with local composition; sealed evidence (so a `DENY` need not republish the material it concerns); quarantine as a reversible state; threshold attestations for high-consequence classes; stake-weighted complaints with slashing for false reports; reuse of `DisputeManager` for evidence-backed disputes; jurisdictional policy bundles as an ordinary issuer with no special status |
| **Mechanisms rejected** | **Global blocklist** — is the censorship mechanism. **Majority vote on content** — converts §12.0a governance into a content authority and violates the §12.0a invariant. **Automated classification at the network layer** — a classifier at a relay is a content oracle and a false-positive engine, and it requires plaintext the relay must not have. **Reputation attached to users** — requires stable user identity, which the anonymity layer exists to prevent |

| **Economic changes** | Complaint bonds and slashing for demonstrably false assertions, in the classes where "false" is objectively decidable (fraud, non-storage). **Not** in the content classes, where it is not decidable and a bond becomes a wealth test for speech |

| **Metrics** | Propagation latency; capacity fraction required for object removal; issuer concentration (Gini or top-1 subscription share) over simulated time; false-positive rate in decidable classes |
| **Milestones** | M2.1 taxonomy and the five local mechanisms built. M2.2 assertion format and composition. M2.3 removal-cost calculation against the real coding parameters. M2.4 issuer-concentration simulation. M2.5 the §29.6 statement written and reviewed |
| **Exit criteria** | E2.1 The five locally-enforceable classes are implemented and tested. E2.2 A build-time test asserts no default subscription ships — falsified by any shipped issuer key. E2.3 The removal-cost figure is published for the deployed coding parameters. E2.4 The concentration simulation is published **including the case where it shows convergence on a dominant issuer**, which is the result that would refute the design |
| **Residual risks** | Market-share re-centralisation: nothing prevents 95 % of operators subscribing to one issuer, which is a global blocklist assembled voluntarily. Encryption does not confer legal safety on storage or exit operators. Sealed evidence is unverifiable by non-subscribers, so an issuer can lie about its reasons |
| **New attacks introduced** | **Assertion flooding** (mitigated by quota and bond in decidable classes). **Subscription inference** — an observer who can probe what a node refuses to serve learns its subscriptions, hence something about its operator's jurisdiction or politics; this is a genuine new leak and is `[NEEDS RESEARCH]`. **Quarantine abuse** as a cheap denial primitive if reversal is slow |

### 29.5 What this workstream does NOT establish

- That abuse is reduced. It establishes who may act and how fast, not outcomes.
- That operators are legally protected. They are not, and the document must not
  imply that a technical measure changes a legal exposure.
- That subscription stays decentralised. §29.4's E2.4 exists because the honest
  expectation is partial convergence.
- Anything about the content classes. Those are §29.6's subject and §29.6's
  answer is that they are not an engineering problem.

### 29.6 Where technical enforcement becomes governance — the explicit statement

```text
  TECHNICAL                                          │  GOVERNANCE
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────
  I refuse to store this shard                       │  You must refuse it
  I refuse to relay for this service                 │  Nobody may relay for it
  I refuse to resolve this name                      │  The name must not resolve
  I publish an assertion                             │  My assertion binds you
  I subscribe to an issuer                           │  Everyone subscribes
  My registrar refuses this registration (§12.0)     │  All registrars refuse it
```

Everything in the left column is implementable, local, and requires no authority.
Everything in the right column requires a party who can compel operators, and
building that party is building the censorship infrastructure — regardless of
whether it is a company, a DAO, or a majority vote. AXON builds the left column
and refuses the right, and the consequence, stated without softening: **AXON has
no mechanism for globally removing anything, including material that essentially
everyone would agree should be removed.** That is a deliberate and permanent
property of the architecture, and any operator or jurisdiction that cannot accept
it should not run a storage or gateway node.

## 30. Workstream 3 — Eclipse Resistance and Network-View Consistency

### 30.1 The problem R14 left open

AXON has no consensus document, so a node has no reference view. The question it
cannot currently answer is not "is this relay real?" — descriptors are signed —
but **"am I being shown all of them?"** Omission is the attack, and signatures do
not detect omission.

### 30.2 The mechanism: view digests, beacon-sampled peers, and a chain anchor

Three layers, in increasing order of cost and of resistance.

**(a) Epoch view digest.** Each node computes a compact digest of the relay set
it observed this epoch and publishes it as a signed record:

```text
  ViewDigest {
    epoch, SRV_epoch
    filter        Bloom or Cuckoo filter over observed NodeIdentity set
    count         cardinality estimate
    sampleRoot    Merkle root over a beacon-selected sub-sample
    signature
  }
```

A node fetches `m` digests from peers *selected by the epoch's SRV rather than by
its own routing table* — the adversary cannot bias a selection it cannot predict
— and compares. Divergence is measurable: `|A △ B| / |A ∪ B|`, and a node whose
symmetric difference against the median peer exceeds a threshold is being
partitioned. Cost is a filter per node per epoch, which is kilobytes.

**(b) Beacon-derived peer sampling.** Random-walk peer sampling biased by the
adversary is the standard eclipse vector. Using `SRV_epoch` to seed which
keyspace regions a node samples makes the sample unpredictable-in-advance and
verifiable-after-the-fact, reusing R13's existing beacon rather than adding one.

**(c) The chain anchor — the part that makes detection survivable.** A node whose
every peer is adversarial cannot detect anything from inside the partition. It
needs a channel the adversary does not control, and **AXON already has one: the
light client.** Anchoring a periodic relay-set commitment on chain gives an
eclipsed node an independent statement of the relay population's size and root:

```text
  epoch e:  relay-set Merkle root + cardinality committed on chain
            ├── a node whose view omits committed relays detects omission
            ├── the commitment is verified through the existing light client,
            │   so an eclipsing adversary must ALSO eclipse chain access —
            │   which is exactly what §31 (P4) prevents
            └── cost: one transaction per epoch, off the request path
```

This is the coupling promised in §26.3, and it is why P3 and P4 are scheduled
together. It is also `[NEEDS RESEARCH]`: who computes the commitment, and how the
network agrees on it without a consensus, is unresolved. The least-bad candidate
is the §12.6 snapshot anchorer pattern — a bonded poster, an optimistic challenge
window, slashing on a proven-wrong root — which makes the poster untrusted rather
than trusted. Whether the relay set is well-defined enough to commit to at all,
given continuous churn, is the open question.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Threat model** | Adversary controls fraction `f` of peer-selection opportunities via keyspace position, bootstrap capture, or network position; wants a victim to see only adversary relays; may combine with a network-level block of honest peers |

### 30.4 What this workstream does NOT establish

- Recovery in a total partition. Detection is achievable; escape requires a
  channel the adversary does not hold.
- That the relay set is a well-defined object. §30.2(c) may fail on exactly this
  point, and if it does, the chain anchor is dropped and `P_ecl` is bounded only
  by (a) and (b).
- Protection against a network-level adversary who blocks honest peers at the IP
  layer rather than out-competing them in the DHT — that is a censorship problem
  and is out of scope for v1 (§6.8).
- Any claim about the *early* network, where `f` is large simply because the
  population is small.
